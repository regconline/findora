Every town and suburb in South Africa is full of capable businesses: the mechanic who has kept the neighbourhood's cars running for twenty years, the young graphic designer working from home, the family-run bakery, the tutor helping matric learners after school. Yet many residents never find them. They default to large chains or to whatever appears first online, even when an excellent local option is a few streets away.

Making local businesses easier to discover is not just convenient. It has real economic and social benefits.

## Money that stays local

When you spend money with a local business, a larger share of that money tends to stay in your community. The owner pays local staff, buys from local suppliers and spends their own income nearby. That spending supports other businesses in turn.

Large national businesses play an important role, but a healthy local economy needs a strong base of small and medium enterprises as well.

## Small businesses create jobs

Small, medium and micro enterprises are a major source of employment in South Africa. When a local business gets more customers, it is often in a position to hire an assistant, take on an apprentice or offer extra shifts. Helping customers find these businesses is one practical way to support job creation.

## The visibility gap

Many small businesses rely entirely on word of mouth. That works well within existing networks, but it makes it hard to reach new customers — people who have just moved to the area, younger customers who search on their phones, or people in neighbouring towns.

Common reasons small businesses stay invisible include:

- No website, or an outdated one.
- Information scattered across social media posts that are hard to search.
- Contact numbers that change or are only shared privately.
- No photos or descriptions of services.

A structured business profile solves many of these problems. It gives the business one clear place to describe what it does, where it operates and how to get in touch.

## Better choices for customers

Customers benefit from discovery too. When you can compare several local providers — their services, prices where published, operating hours and photos — you make better decisions. You may find someone closer, more specialised or more affordable than the first name that came to mind.

Direct contact matters as well. Being able to call or WhatsApp a business straight away means faster answers and a personal relationship from the start.

## Resilience in difficult times

Communities with diverse local businesses tend to cope better with economic shocks. If one large employer closes, a network of small enterprises can help absorb the impact. Local businesses also often adapt quickly — offering deliveries, changing products or serving new needs — when circumstances change.

## Township and rural economies

Township and rural businesses face particular visibility challenges, from inconsistent addresses to limited marketing budgets. Yet they serve millions of customers and represent enormous entrepreneurial energy. Making these businesses searchable — by service, by town and by category — helps them reach customers beyond their immediate streets.

## What you can do

Supporting local businesses doesn't require a big commitment:

1. **Search locally first.** Before defaulting to a familiar brand, see who offers the service near you.
2. **Recommend good businesses.** Tell friends, share profiles and give honest feedback directly to the business.
3. **Encourage businesses to list themselves.** If your favourite local shop or tradesperson isn't easy to find online, let them know about free listing options.
4. **Be a good customer.** Pay on time, communicate clearly and respect agreed arrangements.
