Pricing is one of the hardest decisions for a new service business. Many owners copy a competitor's price or simply guess, then discover months later that they're working hard without making much money. A little structure makes pricing far less stressful.

## Start with your real costs

Before you can set a profitable price, you need to know what it costs to deliver your service. Split your costs into two groups.

**Direct costs** — things you spend for each job:

- Materials and consumables.
- Transport and fuel to and from the job.
- Any subcontractor or helper wages for that job.

**Overheads** — costs you pay whether or not you have work:

- Rent, electricity and water for your premises.
- Phone, data and software subscriptions.
- Insurance, accounting fees and bank charges.
- Equipment repairs and replacement.
- Marketing.

Add up your monthly overheads. This is the amount your work needs to cover before you make any profit.

## Decide what you need to earn

Next, decide what you need to pay yourself each month. Be realistic — include enough for your living costs and some savings. Your business isn't truly profitable if you can't pay yourself.

## Work out your billable hours

You can't bill every hour you work. Time spent travelling, quoting, buying materials, doing admin and marketing is usually unbilled. A common mistake is assuming 40 billable hours a week. Many small service businesses only bill 20 to 30.

Estimate honestly how many hours a month you can actually charge for.

## Calculate a minimum hourly rate

A simple formula:

> **Minimum hourly rate = (monthly overheads + what you need to earn) ÷ billable hours per month**

For example, if your overheads are R8,000, you need to earn R20,000, and you can bill 100 hours a month:

> (R8,000 + R20,000) ÷ 100 = **R280 per hour**

This is your *floor*, not your target. Charging less means you're losing money once you count everything. Add a profit margin on top so the business can grow, save for slow months and replace equipment.

## Then look at the market

Now compare your number with what similar businesses charge in your area. Browse business profiles and published prices, and ask suppliers or other business owners what's typical.

- If your calculated price is **far above** the market, look for ways to reduce costs or increase billable time, or target customers who value premium service.
- If it's **far below** the market, you may be underpricing — raise your prices.

## Choose a pricing model

Different services suit different models:

- **Fixed price** — clear and easy for customers, ideal for well-defined jobs (like a wheel alignment or a standard logo design).
- **Starting from** — shows a minimum while allowing for extra work.
- **Hourly rate** — suits work where the time needed is uncertain.
- **Quotation required** — for complex or custom jobs that need an assessment first.

## Don't forget VAT

If your business is VAT-registered, make it clear whether your prices include VAT. Consumers generally expect prices to include VAT; business clients may prefer to see it separately. Ask your accountant how this applies to you.

## Communicate value, not just price

Customers compare more than numbers. Explain what's included, mention guarantees, qualifications and response times, and show photos of your work. A slightly higher price with clear value often wins over the cheapest option.

## Review your prices regularly

Costs rise every year. Review your prices at least annually and whenever a major cost (like fuel or materials) changes. Give existing customers notice of increases, and apply new prices to new quotes first.

## In summary

Know your costs, decide what you need to earn, be honest about billable time, calculate a minimum rate, compare with the market, and communicate your value clearly. Pricing confidently is one of the fastest ways to make a small business more sustainable.
